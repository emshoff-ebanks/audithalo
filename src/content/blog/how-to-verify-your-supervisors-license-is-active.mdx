## Why this is on you to check

Nobody tells you when your supervisor's license lapses. There's no
notification that goes out to supervisees when a renewal deadline is missed,
no automatic flag on your file, and no reason a normal supervision session
would look any different than the one before it. You show up, you talk
through your cases, you log the hour. The credential problem sits quietly in
the background until someone checks it against a calendar, and that someone
is usually you, months or years later, at the worst possible time to find
out.

That's the risk covered in [our companion post on what happens to your hours
if your supervisor's credential lapses](/blog/supervisor-lost-license-do-my-hours-count):
in states like North Carolina, a lapsed supervisor credential can invalidate
the hours logged under it, even if nothing about the supervision itself was
deficient. This post is the other half of that problem. It's the manual
check you can run yourself, today, in a few minutes, to know where you
actually stand instead of assuming.

## The good news: this is mostly a public record

Most state licensing boards run a free, public license-lookup tool. You
don't need a login, a fee, or a formal request to use it. You type in your
supervisor's name or license number, and the board's own database tells you
what it has on file.

Two examples worth naming directly, since we verified both are live and
current:

**North Carolina** runs its verification tool through the NC Board of
Licensed Clinical Mental Health Counselors at
[portal.ncblcmhc.org/verification/search.aspx](https://portal.ncblcmhc.org/verification/search.aspx).
Search by name, and the board's own disclaimer is worth reading closely: a
name showing up in the search does not by itself mean the license is active
and in good standing. You have to open the full record to see status,
expiration, and any disciplinary action.

**Washington** handles this through the Department of Health's statewide
Provider Credential Search at
[doh.wa.gov/licenses-permits-and-certificates/provider-credential-or-facility-search](https://doh.wa.gov/licenses-permits-and-certificates/provider-credential-or-facility-search).
It covers all licensed health professions in the state, including mental
health counselors, and shows license status plus any enforcement actions on
record since 1998.

If your state isn't one of these two, the tool almost certainly still
exists. Search "[your state] [licensing board name] license lookup" or
"license verification," and look for a `.gov` domain or your board's own
site rather than a third-party aggregator that resells the same data with
ads around it. Your state's [supervision requirements page](/states) on
AuditHalo lists the issuing board for each credential we track, which is a
faster way to find the right board than guessing from the license type
alone.

## What to actually look at once you find the record

A green "Active" badge is the least useful piece of information on the page.
Here's what actually tells you something.

**The expiration date, not just the status word.** A license can show
"active" right up until the day it lapses, and some lookup tools update on a
delay. Check the expiration date against today's date yourself. If it's
close, or already past, that's worth a direct question to your supervisor
before you log another session.

**Whether it's the specific supervisory credential, not just any license in
the field.** This is the mistake that catches people off guard. A supervisor
can hold a perfectly valid, perfectly active counseling license and still
not be qualified to supervise you, because your state requires a distinct
supervisory credential on top of it. North Carolina requires the LCMHCS
designation specifically, not just an LCMHC. An active LCMHC alone doesn't
satisfy the requirement. Washington requires the underlying license (LMHC or
an equivalent like LMFT, LICSW, or licensed psychologist) plus good standing
for the two prior years, so the lookup needs to confirm both the license
type and how long it's been continuously valid, not just today's status.
Read the credential type on the record carefully, and compare it against
what your state's rule actually requires, not what sounds close enough.

**Disciplinary action or restrictions.** Most board lookup tools show more
than a status word if you open the full record: any current restrictions,
consent orders, or disciplinary history tied to the license. A license can
be technically active and still carry a restriction that matters for
whether your supervisor is qualified to supervise you right now. Don't stop
at the summary line. Open the detail view.

**Whether the name and license number match exactly.** Common names produce
multiple search results. Confirm you're looking at your actual supervisor's
record, not someone else with a similar name, before you draw any
conclusion from it.

## Building this into a habit, not a one-time check

Checking once, when supervision starts, answers exactly one question: was
your supervisor qualified on day one. It tells you nothing about six months
from now.

Put a recurring reminder on your calendar. Quarterly is a reasonable
cadence, it takes a few minutes, and it catches a lapse or a new
disciplinary action long before a board audit would force the question. Save
a screenshot or a note with the date you checked. If a board ever asks you
to reconstruct your timeline, "I checked this quarterly and here's the
record" is a far stronger answer than "I assumed it was fine."

If you're an HR admin or practice manager tracking this across a whole
roster of supervisees rather than just your own supervision, the same logic
applies at scale, except now you're checking multiple supervisors across
multiple renewal cycles, which is exactly the kind of thing that falls
through the cracks when it's done by memory instead of by system. For the
full sequence of what a board actually asks for when it reviews a file, see
[how to prepare for a counseling board audit](/blog/how-to-prepare-for-a-counseling-board-audit).
