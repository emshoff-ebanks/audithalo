## One supervisor, two supervisees, one session

Triadic supervision is a specific format: one supervisor meets with two
supervisees at the same time, in the same session. It isn't a compromise
between individual and group supervision in some vague, informal sense. It's
a named category with its own definition, and the definition is precise
because licensing boards use it to decide whether a session counts toward
the individual-supervision hours a candidate needs to log.

The format has been part of counselor training for a while. CACREP approved
triadic supervision as a recognized alternative to strictly individual
supervision for practicum and internship students back in 2001, and it's
common enough in graduate programs that most supervisees will sit in at
least one triadic session before they ever apply for a license. The
complication isn't whether triadic supervision exists. It's whether the
board that will eventually review your hours agrees to count it the same
way your graduate program did.

## How it differs from individual and group supervision

Individual supervision is one supervisor, one supervisee. Group supervision
is one supervisor with a room of supervisees, usually capped somewhere
between six and twelve depending on the state. Triadic supervision sits at
exactly two supervisees, no more, no fewer, and that specific headcount is
what makes it a distinct category rather than a small group.

The practical difference shows up in attention, not just arithmetic. In an
individual session, every minute of feedback goes to one person's caseload.
In a triadic session, the supervisor is dividing time and attention between
two people's cases in the same block, which is closer to individual
supervision than a six-person group but isn't the same thing. That's the
exact tension boards are trying to resolve when they write a rule about
whether triadic sessions count as individual, group, or something in
between.

## Why the classification affects your individual-hours ratio

Most states that regulate pre-licensure supervision set a floor on how much
of your total supervision has to be individual rather than group, tracked as
a running percentage across everything you've logged. [Individual vs. group
supervision requirements](/blog/individual-vs-group-supervision-requirements)
covers how that floor works in North Carolina and Washington in detail.
Triadic supervision matters here because its classification, individual or
group, changes which side of that ratio a session lands on.

Get the classification wrong and the effect isn't visible right away. A
supervisor who runs a standing triadic arrangement with two supervisees for
efficiency reasons might be logging sessions that both people assume count
as individual. If the state doesn't recognize that assumption, or recognizes
it only with a condition neither supervisor nor supervisee has satisfied,
every one of those sessions is actually landing in the group column. The
individual-hours share can slide for months before anyone checks the
cumulative math, because nothing about any single session looked wrong.

## North Carolina: an attestation, not an automatic pass

North Carolina's LCMHCA rule (21 NCAC 53), overseen by the [NC Board of
Licensed Clinical Mental Health Counselors](https://www.ncblcmhc.org/licensure/),
is explicit about triadic supervision. A session with one supervisor and two
supervisees can count toward the individual-supervision requirement, but only
if the supervisor attests that the session provided individual-quality
attention to each supervisee. Without that attestation, the session doesn't
get individual credit by default.

That's a meaningful distinction from a state simply saying "triadic counts as
individual." North Carolina's rule is conditional. The attestation is a
specific act the supervisor has to take, not something implied by having run
the session at all. AuditHalo's default, in the absence of a recorded
attestation, is to treat a triadic session in North Carolina as group. That's
the conservative read of a rule that makes the individual-credit path
optional rather than automatic, and it means a supervisee's individual-hours
share won't get inflated based on session headcount alone.

## Other states: two different answers, and one state that doesn't use the term at all

North Carolina isn't the only state whose rules speak to a two-supervisee
session, but the other two that do handle it differently, and neither
matches North Carolina's attestation model exactly.

Ohio's rule doesn't use the word "triadic" at all. Instead, its definition of
individual supervision permits up to two supervisees in the same session by
default, no attestation, no separate category. What another state would call
a triadic session, Ohio simply calls individual, because its definition of
that term was written to include two people from the start.

California's APCC rule does use the term directly, and its rule reads as
unconditional: a triadic session, one supervisor and two supervisees,
counts as individual supervision in California without an attestation step.
Worth flagging, though: AuditHalo's California rule file is currently marked
preliminary and pending a licensed-supervisor review, so treat that
provision as a strong lead rather than a fully verified fact until it's
confirmed against the California Board of Behavioral Sciences's current
regulations directly.

That's three states, three different postures, none of which line up with
each other. If your state isn't North Carolina, Ohio, or California, don't
assume a triadic session counts as individual just because one of these
three does it that way. Check your own state's rule, or ask your supervisor
to confirm it with the board directly, before you build a supervision
schedule around triadic sessions.
