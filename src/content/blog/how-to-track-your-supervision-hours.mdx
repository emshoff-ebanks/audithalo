## Why a plain hour count isn't enough

Most supervisees start with a spreadsheet: a date column, a duration column,
a running total. It feels complete, and for a while it is. Then a board
audit or a licensure application asks a question the spreadsheet was never
built to answer, and the gap becomes obvious.

A total hour count answers one question: how many hours have you logged. It
doesn't answer whether the mix of individual and group hours meets your
state's floor, whether your supervisor's credential was valid on every date
you logged, or whether you kept pace with the required cadence instead of
front-loading hours in a few busy months and going quiet for the rest. Those
three things are exactly what a board checks, and a spreadsheet that only
sums a column has nothing to say about any of them.

This applies whether you're the one logging your own hours or you're a
supervisor watching a roster of several supervisees at once. A supervisor
who only tracks each supervisee's running total has the same blind spot,
multiplied by however many people are on the roster.

## The number that has to be tracked continuously, not totaled at the end

The clearest example is the individual-supervision share: the percentage of
your supervision hours that has to be one-on-one rather than group.

[North Carolina](/states/nc-lcmhca) requires that at least 75% of your total
supervision hours be individual, and the rule is enforced as a rolling
fraction once you've logged 200 or more practice hours, not a box you check
once at the end. A supervisee who logs a clean individual session every week
in month one and then joins a busy group rotation for months two through six
can fall under 75% without a single session looking wrong on its own. The
problem isn't any one entry. It's the running average, and nothing about a
simple total-hours column shows you that average unless you calculate it
yourself, every time.

[Washington](/states/wa-lmhca) sets the bar even higher: 100% of the
required 100 hours must be "immediate supervision," meaning a meeting with
the supervisor and no more than two candidates. Group sessions of three to
six people don't count toward that total at all, regardless of how many you
attend. A supervisee tracking only a total-hours number could sit at 100
logged supervision hours and still be short of licensure, because a chunk of
those hours were group sessions that never counted toward the requirement in
the first place.

Two states, two different numbers (75% and 100%), and in both cases the
number that matters isn't visible in a simple sum. It has to be calculated
as a share, and recalculated every time a new session gets logged, or the
gap doesn't show up until someone finally does the math by hand, usually
much later than anyone would like.

The same logic applies to a supervisor's credential. North Carolina requires
the supervisor to hold the LCMHCS credential specifically, and Washington
accepts a defined list of license types (LMHC, LMFT, LICSW, licensed
psychologist, licensed psychiatrist, or psychiatric nurse practitioner) as
long as the supervisor has been licensed without restriction for the prior
two years. A total-hours spreadsheet has no field for "was this credential
still valid on this date." If it lapses partway through a supervision
relationship, the only way to catch it is to check the credential against
each session date directly, which almost nobody does until an audit forces
the question.

## What to actually log, and when

**Log the same day, not from memory weeks later.** A note written right
after the session captures the real start time, the real duration, and what
was actually discussed. A note reconstructed from a calendar entry three
weeks later captures an approximation, and approximations are exactly what a
board audit is designed to catch.

**Record session type explicitly.** Individual, group, or triadic, not just
"supervision." Each type gets weighted differently against your state's
requirements (North Carolina, for instance, counts two hours of group
supervision as one hour toward the practice-hour block requirement), and a
log entry that doesn't distinguish types can't be checked against any of
these ratios at all. Read more on how to structure a single session record
in [how to document a supervision session](/blog/how-to-document-a-supervision-session),
and on how the individual-versus-group distinction plays out across states
in [individual vs. group supervision requirements](/blog/individual-vs-group-supervision-requirements).

**Capture the supervisor's credential at the time of the session, not as
assumed.** Write down the specific credential (LCMHCS, LMHC, whatever your
state requires) as it stood on that date. If you're a supervisor managing
several supervisees, this means checking your own credential status
periodically too, since a lapse on your end invalidates hours for everyone
you're supervising, not just one file.

**Track the running individual-share percentage, not just the total.** After
each entry, recalculate the fraction: individual hours divided by total
supervision hours logged so far. Watch it the way you'd watch a bank
balance, not the way you'd check a to-do list once a year.

**Reconcile against your state's total periodically.** Set a monthly or
quarterly checkpoint and compare your running numbers against the actual
requirement: total supervision hours, individual share, cadence, and how
much of your duration window remains. Don't wait for the end of the
obligation period to find out where you stand. By then, a gap that would
have taken a month to close might take a year.

**Keep dual signatures on every entry.** A record only the supervisor signs,
or only the supervisee fills in, is weaker evidence than one both parties
attest to. If a correction is needed later, it should appear as a new dated
entry, not a quiet edit to the original.

## FAQ

**Is a spreadsheet enough to track supervision hours?**
A spreadsheet can hold a total, but most state requirements aren't a single
total. They're a mix of a practice-hour count, a supervision-hour count, an
individual-share percentage, a cadence requirement, and a duration window,
all tracked at once. A spreadsheet can be made to do this, but it has to be
maintained by hand every time a new session is logged, and the individual-
share calculation in particular tends to get skipped until someone remembers
to run it.

**How often should I reconcile my supervision hours against my state's
requirement?**
Monthly or quarterly, not once at the end. The individual-share floor and
the cadence requirement are both running numbers, and a gap caught in month
three takes a few extra sessions to close. The same gap caught in month
thirty of a sixty-month window may not leave enough time left to fix.

**What's the difference between tracking hours as a supervisee versus as a
supervisor?**
A supervisee tracks one running set of numbers: their own individual share,
cadence, and total hours against their own state's rule. A supervisor
tracks the same set of numbers multiplied across every person on their
roster, plus their own credential status, since a lapse on the supervisor's
side affects every supervisee they're currently supervising.

**Does logging a session the same day actually matter to a board?**
Yes. A contemporaneous record, one created at or near the time of the
session, carries more weight than one reconstructed later from memory or a
calendar. Boards that review documentation closely can often tell when a
block of entries was created in one sitting rather than logged as sessions
happened, and that pattern alone can raise questions even when the
underlying hours are legitimate.
