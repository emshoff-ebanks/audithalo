## What "rejected" actually means

A board doesn't usually reject an entire supervision log. What actually happens is
narrower and, in a way, worse: a specific block of hours — the ones supervised by
a since-lapsed credential, or logged at a setting that was never approved — is
ruled invalid, while the rest of the log stands. You don't find out which hours
until someone checks each one against the rule that was in force when it was
logged. That's the whole reason a [board audit prep](/blog/how-to-prepare-for-a-counseling-board-audit)
routine has to check the specifics, not just the totals.

## The three common reasons hours get invalidated after the fact

**A supervisor credential that lapsed.** If your supervisor's license or
supervisory designation expired, was suspended, or was never the qualifying type
to begin with, sessions supervised during that gap are at risk — even if
everything else about the session was fine. [North Carolina](/states/nc-lcmhca)
requires the supervisor to hold the LCMHCS credential specifically; a supervisor
who let that credential lapse for even a few months creates a gap that often isn't
noticed until someone reconciles dates against the license record.

**A setting that was never approved, or stopped being approved.**
[Louisiana](/states/la-plpc) is the sharpest example in either direction: the
practice setting must be board-approved before hours accrue there, and if you
change settings without notifying the Board within 30 days, every hour earned at
that setting is forfeited outright (LAC 46:LX §603(A)(6)(d)) — not reduced,
not flagged for review, gone. The statute is explicit that retroactive hours
are prohibited (§605(A)(3)): there is no path to recover them after the fact.

**A supervision plan or contract that wasn't actually approved yet.** The
difference between "submitted" and "approved" matters enormously here. Several
states, including North Carolina, treat any hour logged before the board approves
the filed contract as not having started the clock at all — regardless of how
good the supervision itself was.

## What you can and can't fix retroactively

This is the split that matters most, and it's genuinely different state to state.

Some gaps are recoverable: if a supervisor's credential lapsed for a defined
window and you can identify exactly which sessions fall inside it, some boards
will let you re-supervise just that block rather than restart the whole
obligation. [Arizona](/states/az-lac), for instance, allows up to six supervisors
to contribute to a single application specifically because supervision
arrangements change — the rule anticipates transitions rather than punishing them,
as long as each supervisor was properly credentialed for their portion.

Other gaps are not recoverable at all. Louisiana's setting-forfeiture rule is the
clearest example: the statute doesn't distinguish between "the setting was
approved two weeks late" and "the setting was never approved" — both forfeit the
hours. When a rule is written as a hard forfeiture rather than a correctable
deficiency, there's no remediation path, and the only real defense is never
triggering it.

## How this affects your licensure timeline

Most states cap the total window for accumulating hours somewhere between four
and six years. Hours you have to redo still take real calendar time — you can't
compress twelve months of required cadence into six weeks just because you're
behind. The risk compounds the later a gap is caught: found in year one, it's an
inconvenience; found in year five of a six-year window, it can mean the
difference between licensure on schedule and having to request an extension the
board isn't obligated to grant.

## How to respond if a board flags your hours

Ask for the specific citation, not just the finding. "Some of your hours don't
meet requirements" is not actionable; "hours logged between March and June don't
qualify under [admin code section] because the supervising credential lapsed on
March 2" is. Once you have the citation, check your own evidence against that
exact provision before assuming the board is right — or before assuming they're
wrong. Boards audit a lot of files and do occasionally cite the wrong window or
the wrong rule version; you can only catch that by comparing dates directly
against the cited requirement, not the summary of it.
