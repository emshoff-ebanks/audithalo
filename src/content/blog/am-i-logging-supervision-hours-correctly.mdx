## Six questions, before a board asks them

If you're a supervisee, you probably have a log somewhere: a spreadsheet, a
notebook, a form your supervisor emails you at month's end. What you
probably don't have is a clear answer to whether that log would survive
someone else looking at it.

This isn't a how-to guide for setting up a tracking system. [That guide
already exists](/blog/how-to-track-your-supervision-hours). This is six
questions to run against the log you already have, right now, to find the
gap before a board finds it for you. Each is tied to an actual state
supervision rule, not generic advice.

### 1. Is your supervision contract actually filed and approved, not just submitted?

In North Carolina, hours logged before your supervision contract is on file
with the board don't count toward the 3,000-hour requirement for [LCMHCA
licensure](/states/nc-lcmhca), full stop. The clock starts the day the
contract is approved, not the day you mailed it.

Texas runs the same logic for its [LPC-Associate
pathway](/states/tx-lpc-associate): a Supervisory Agreement has to be on
file before any hour is creditable, and the LPC-Associate license itself
has to be active too. Two filings, both required, both before the clock
starts.

Check: do you have a dated confirmation that your contract was *approved*,
not just a copy of what you submitted? "I sent it in" and "it's on file"
are different facts, and only one protects your hours.

### 2. Is your supervisor's credential still current, on every date you logged?

North Carolina requires your supervisor to hold the LCMHCS credential
specifically, and no other license type substitutes for it. If it lapses
mid-relationship, hours logged under the lapsed credential are the ones a
board questions first.

Texas requires something similar but distinct: an active LPC license plus
a separate LPC-Supervisor designation, earned through five years of
licensure and a 40-hour board-approved course. A supervisor who lost the
Supervisor designation but kept a standard LPC license still isn't
qualified to sign your hours, even though nothing about their license
looks wrong at a glance.

Check: do you know the exact date your supervisor's credential was last
verified, and is it recent, not just "sometime last year when we started"?

### 3. Is your individual-to-group ratio drifting, not just your total hours?

A running hour total can look healthy while a ratio underneath it quietly
fails. North Carolina requires at least 75% of total supervision hours to
be individual, enforced as a rolling fraction once you've logged 200 or
more practice hours. A supervisee who front-loads individual sessions early
and then joins a busy group rotation for a few months can drop under 75%
without any single session looking like a problem. The issue is the
average, and a total-hours column says nothing about an average unless
someone calculates it.

Check: pull your individual hours and total supervision hours as two
separate numbers right now, and divide. If you can't produce both in under
a minute, that's the finding. See [individual vs. group supervision
requirements](/blog/individual-vs-group-supervision-requirements) for how
this ratio gets misread in practice.

### 4. Are your sessions signed the same day, or batch-signed weeks later?

A note written the day of the session captures the real start time and
what was actually covered. A block of entries signed in one sitting, three
weeks later, looks different to anyone reviewing the record closely. No
state writes this into its admin code word for word, but it shows up
everywhere signatures and dates are part of the evidence record, which is
nearly every state.

Check: look at the signature timestamps on your last ten entries, not the
session dates. Several sharing a signature date that isn't close to any
session date is a batch-signing pattern worth fixing before it becomes a
habit.

### 5. Are you tracking the cadence requirement, not just the total?

Totals and cadence are different things, and states enforce both. North
Carolina requires at least one hour of individual supervision every 14
days, separate from the 75% ratio. Texas requires four full hours of
supervision every month, with at least one individual. A supervisee who
hits their yearly total but goes six weeks without a session mid-year has
broken cadence in both states, even though the year-end total looks fine.

Check: find the longest gap between two consecutive sessions in your log.
If it's longer than your state's cadence window, a healthy total at
year-end won't undo it.

### 6. Is your direct-client-contact split tracked separately from your total practice hours?

Total practice hours and direct client contact hours aren't the same
number, and conflating them is a common self-audit miss. North Carolina
requires at least 2,000 of the 3,000 total practice hours to be direct
client contact (live counseling with individuals, groups, or families),
with the remainder allowed as indirect work like documentation or case
consultation. Texas sets a similar floor: at least 1,500 of its 3,000 total
hours must be direct client contact.

Check: if your log has one running practice-hours number and no separate
column for direct versus indirect work, you can't answer whether you meet
this floor. Fix it well before an application deadline, not the week of one.

## What a "yes" to all six actually requires

Answering all six well means keeping several numbers current at once: a
contract status, a credential expiration, a rolling ratio, a signature
timestamp, a cadence gap, and a direct-contact split. None is hard to check
alone; doing all six for every entry without letting one go stale is what
turns a five-minute self-audit into a chore most people quietly stop doing
after a few months.

If a self-audit surfaces a real gap, particularly on the contract or
credential questions, the fix isn't more logging discipline. It's getting
the paperwork corrected with your supervisor and the board before more
hours accrue on top of it. For what a formal board review looks for beyond
these six items, see [how to prepare for a counseling board
audit](/blog/how-to-prepare-for-a-counseling-board-audit); for a fuller
walk-through against your own file, see the [counseling supervision audit
checklist](/counseling-supervision-audit-checklist).

## FAQ

**How do I know if my supervision contract is actually approved, not just submitted?**
Check for a dated confirmation from the board, not a copy of what you sent
in. If you only have proof of submission, treat your hours as unconfirmed
until you have proof of approval.

**What's the difference between a total hour count and a ratio requirement?**
A total is a single running sum. A ratio, like North Carolina's 75%
individual-supervision floor, has to hold true across all logged hours at
once. A log that only tracks totals can't show you the ratio without a
separate, manual calculation.

**Can a lapsed supervisor credential really invalidate hours I already logged?**
Yes, in most states. If your supervisor's required credential (LCMHCS in
North Carolina, the LPC-Supervisor designation in Texas) lapses mid-
relationship, the hours logged while it was lapsed are the ones a board
challenges first.

**Does same-day signing actually matter if the hours are accurate?**
It matters to how the record reads, even when the hours are legitimate. A
signature entered near the time of the session is harder to question than
a batch of entries all signed on one later date.

**How often should I run this six-question self-check?**
Monthly, at minimum. A gap caught a month in takes a session or two to
close. The same gap caught two years in, near a licensure application, may
not leave enough time to fix at all.
