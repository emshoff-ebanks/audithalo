## The one fact that changes everything about a Florida timeline

Most state supervision windows are forgiving in one specific way: if you fall behind,
you can usually make up ground later. Florida's RMHCI registration is not built that
way. It's valid for 60 months, and it cannot be renewed.

We didn't take that from our own rule file at face value. Before publishing this, we
checked it directly against Florida Statute 491.0045 and the Florida Board of Clinical
Social Work, Marriage and Family Therapy, and Mental Health Counseling's own site. The
board states it plainly: "A Registered Intern registration, pursuant to section
491.0045, Florida Statutes, is valid for five (5) years and cannot be renewed." The
statute itself puts a number on it: registrations issued after March 31, 2017 expire
60 months after the date they're issued.

That's a five-year clock with no renewal application waiting on the other side of it.
Everything else about your Florida timeline, your hours, your supervision, your exam,
has to fit inside that window.

## What "non-renewable" means once you're in it

If you've worked in a state with renewable credentials, "non-renewable" might not
register as the alarming word it is. There's no form to file at year four, no fee that
buys you another term, no administrative renewal that quietly keeps your registration
alive while you finish up. When the 60 months are gone, the registration is gone with
them.

What happens next depends entirely on how much you've completed by that point, and the
board's guidance lays out four distinct paths, not one:

**If you've completed your two years of supervised experience and passed your exam,**
you apply for full licensure. This is the outcome everyone's aiming for, and if you get
there before expiration, the non-renewable clock stops mattering.

**If you've completed the two-year minimum but still need time on other requirements,**
you may qualify for a provisional license, which gives you more room without forcing a
full restart.

**If you haven't reached the two-year minimum when your registration expires,** the
board's own language is unambiguous: you submit a new application for Registered
Intern registration and start over as a new intern, under whatever requirements are in
effect at the time you reapply. That last part matters. Rules change. The 1,500-hour
total and the supervision structure you started under may not be the ones you restart
under.

**There is also a narrow escape hatch,** and it's worth knowing about precisely
because it's easy to assume it will save you when it won't.

## The one-time hardship extension, and why it isn't a safety net

Section 491.0045(6) authorizes the board to grant a one-time exception in emergency or
hardship cases. The implementing rule, Fla. Admin. Code R. 64B4-3.0085, sets the actual
length: if approved, the extension runs 24 months past the original expiration date.
That sounds like real breathing room, and for the right candidate it is. But the
eligibility bar is specific:

- You must have already passed the theory and practice exam.
- You must have completed your educational requirements, including your practicum hours.
- You must be able to document circumstances beyond your control that prevented you
  from finishing on time.
- You must file the request at least 30 days before your registration expires.

Notice what that excludes. It doesn't help someone who's behind on hours because the
timeline was tight from the start. It doesn't help someone who hasn't sat for the exam
yet. And the 30-day filing deadline means you can't discover this option the week your
registration lapses and still use it. If you're not tracking your own countdown, you
can miss the extension window before you even know you needed it.

## The numbers we're not stating with full confidence yet

The 60-month, non-renewable structure is the fact we verified directly against the
statute and the board's guidance for this post. The rest of Florida's requirements,
the total hour count, the supervision hour split, the face-to-face minimum, come from
Florida's currently tracked rule, which is marked preliminary in our system pending a
licensed Florida supervisor's review of the current administrative code.

Per that preliminary rule, Florida requires 1,500 hours of supervised post-master's
experience and 100 hours of supervision, with at least half of the supervision hours
delivered individually. We're stating those numbers with that qualifier deliberately.
A five-year deadline you can't extend is exactly the kind of situation where getting a
number wrong costs someone real time, so we'd rather tell you plainly that these are
pending confirmation than present them with certainty we don't have yet.

## Why this is a timing problem, not a hours problem

Florida's combination is unusual: 1,500 total hours is on the lower end of what states
require, but the five-year, non-renewable ceiling is the least forgiving deadline
structure we track. That combination flips the usual risk. In most states, the
question is whether you'll accumulate enough hours. In Florida, the question is
whether you'll accumulate them, complete your supervision, and pass the exam before a
clock that doesn't pause for anything runs out.

That's a different kind of risk to manage, and it's one that rewards early visibility
far more than it rewards working harder in month 58.

## What to check on your own registration right now

If you're an RMHCI in Florida, or you supervise one, three dates are worth writing
down today rather than trusting to memory:

**The exact issuance date on your registration letter.** Not the date you started
your job, not the date your supervisor signed your supervision plan. The date the
board issued your registration is the date the 60-month clock started, and it's the
only date that determines your expiration.

**The date 24 months out from issuance.** That's the earliest point you could
plausibly qualify for full licensure or a provisional license, assuming your hours and
supervision are on pace. If you're well past that date and still short of the two-year
minimum, that's a signal worth acting on rather than watching pass.

**The date 30 days before expiration.** That's your last chance to file for the
one-time hardship extension if you need it and qualify for it. Missing that date
doesn't just cost you the extension, it removes an option you may not realize you had
until it's gone.

None of these dates require a call to the board to find. They're all derived from a
single piece of paper you already have. The work is remembering to check them before
they matter instead of after.
