## Two different jobs

A supervision log template and supervision software are trying to solve two
different problems, and confusing them is where most of the frustration
starts. A template's job is to give a session a place to live: a date, a
duration, a note about what was covered. Software's job is to check that
session against a rule, prove who was in the room, and produce something a
third party can independently verify years later.

Both are legitimate answers. Which one you need depends on where you are,
not on which one is objectively better. This is a decision framework, not
an argument that a template is wrong.

If you haven't started tracking yet, the fastest way to see the difference
in practice is to grab a [free supervision log template](/supervision-log-template)
and use it for your first few sessions. It's a fine place to begin.

## When a template is the right call

A template earns its keep in a specific set of conditions, and it's worth
naming them plainly instead of hand-waving past them.

**You're early in a single supervisory relationship.** One supervisor, one
supervisee, a normal caseload, no unusual circumstances. There isn't much
for software to check yet, and a clean spreadsheet or printed form does the
job.

**Your state's rule is simple and you already know it cold.** Some states
have a short, stable rule set: a total hour count, a ratio requirement, not
much else. If you've supervised for years under that rule and know it by
memory, a template's blank spots don't hurt you, because you're mentally
filling them in yourself.

**Audit risk is low and distant.** If your supervisee is a year or more from
applying for licensure, there's no immediate proof-of-record moment
approaching. You have time to migrate later if things get more complex.

**You're diligent about the parts a template can't check.** A template
doesn't flag a lapsed supervisor credential or a missed filing deadline.
If you're personally tracking those facts some other way, checking your own
license renewal date, confirming your contract was filed, then the template
is doing the one job you're asking of it: holding a record.

None of this is a knock on templates. A supervision log template is free,
familiar, and requires no account, no login, and no learning curve. For a
narrow set of circumstances, that's exactly the right trade-off.

## Where a template stops being enough

The same template that worked fine in month one starts to strain under a
few specific conditions. These are the actual trigger points, not a vague
"eventually you'll outgrow it."

**More than a couple of supervisees.** A template scales linearly with your
attention. Two supervisees means checking two sets of hours, two ratios,
two contract statuses, by hand, every time something changes. Five
supervisees means five. Nobody catches every drift by eye at that scale,
and the ones that get missed are usually the ones that mattered.

**More than one state.** Different states define individual versus group
supervision differently, set different minimum ratios, and treat contract
filing timelines differently. A template built around one state's rule
quietly stops matching the rule the moment a supervisee's state, or the
state a session took place in, is different from the one the template
assumes.

**An audit or licensure application is approaching.** This is the moment a
board looks closely at what you've been keeping. A template that's been
fine for two years can fail in the last month if a credential lapsed
partway through and nobody flagged it, or if a session in month four was
logged before the supervision contract was approved. Boards check for
exactly these gaps.

**You need tamper-evidence, not just a record.** A board doesn't just want
to know a session happened. It wants proof the record hasn't been edited
since. A spreadsheet cell or a filled-in PDF form has no mechanism for
proving that. Anyone with edit access can change a date, and there's no way
for an outside party to check whether they did.

**You need the log to check itself.** A template stores what you type into
it. It has no idea what your state's rule currently requires, whether it
changed since you started, or whether your own supervisor is still
credentialed to sign off on your hours. Catching that requires something
that evaluates each entry against a live rule, not a form that just holds
your entries.

We covered the mechanics of the spreadsheet failure mode in more depth in
[AuditHalo vs. a spreadsheet](/blog/audithalo-vs-a-spreadsheet). That post
is about one specific template format. This one is about the broader
decision: template versus dedicated software, whatever form the template
takes.

## What software adds

The gap between a template and software isn't about polish. It's about what
each one is capable of doing with the same information.

AuditHalo's [evidence packages](/evidence-packages) illustrate the concrete
difference. Each supervision session requires both supervisor and supervisee
to sign with an explicit intent confirmation, not a checkbox that can be
clicked by accident or filled in by one party alone. The signature captures
the signer's name, credential, role, a timestamp, and an IP address. Once
both signatures are in, the package is sealed: SHA-256 hashed, with the hash
covering the full signed content, so a change to a single field after the
fact would be detectable. Anyone holding the package, board, employer, or
auditor, can paste the verify-URL into a browser and confirm the hash still
matches. No account, no login, no phone call to you.

Underneath that, AuditHalo checks each session against the current version
of your state's rule at the moment it's logged: the individual-supervision
floor, the supervisor's credential status, whether the setting was approved,
whether the contract was filed and approved before the session happened.
When something drifts, a flag surfaces in the dashboard, often months
before an audit would ever surface it on its own.

A template can't do either of these things, not because it's poorly
designed, but because that was never its job. It's a place to write things
down. Software is a place where what you write down gets checked and
proven.

## The contract is a separate decision

It's worth separating two things that get lumped together: the supervision
log and the supervision contract. A [clinical supervision contract
template](/blog/clinical-supervision-contract-template) defines the terms
of the relationship, scope, supervision modality, expected hours, and in a
growing number of states, has to be filed and approved by the board before
any hour counts. That's a different document doing a different job than
the log that records individual sessions after the contract is in place.
You can use a contract template and log software together, or a log
template and neither, in almost any combination. Deciding on one doesn't
automatically decide the other.

## A short way to decide

If you're supervising one person, in one state, with a rule you know well,
and you're personally checking the things a template can't, a template is a
reasonable choice today. If you're supervising several people, across more
than one state, approaching an audit or a licensure deadline, or if you've
already had a close call where your record couldn't answer a question
cleanly, that's the point to move to something that checks the rule for you
and seals the proof.

Neither answer is permanent. Plenty of supervisors start with a template and
move to software once their roster or their risk changes. The mistake isn't
picking the "wrong" one at the start. It's staying with a template past the
point where the conditions that made it adequate have already changed.
