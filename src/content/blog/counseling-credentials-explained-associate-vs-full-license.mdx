If you're trying to figure out what your state calls the credential you hold
right now, or what you're working toward, you've probably noticed the
alphabet soup doesn't help. LCMHCA, APCC, PLPC, LMHCA, LPC-Associate, RMHCI,
LAC, LACMH. Different letters, different boards, and at first glance, no
obvious pattern connecting any of them.

There is a pattern. Every state we cover runs the same two-stage structure:
a temporary, supervised, restricted-practice credential that lets you see
clients while accumulating hours, followed by a full, independent-practice
license once you clear the hour threshold and pass the exam. What changes
from state to state is the name of each stage, the number of hours required,
and, in a handful of states, whether a full license is actually the last
rung on the ladder. This post lays out the shared shape, then gives you
real numbers across eight states so you can see where your state lands.

## The shape that repeats in every state

Strip away the acronyms and every state's licensure pathway follows the
same three-part structure.

**Stage one: education and exam.** A master's degree in counseling or a
closely related field, usually from a CACREP-accredited program or its
state-recognized equivalent, plus a national exam (the NCE or NCMHCE in
most states). This part is close to identical everywhere.

**Stage two: the associate credential.** Once you clear stage one, the
state issues a credential that lets you practice under supervision while
you log hours toward independent practice. This is the LCMHCA in North
Carolina, the APCC in California, the PLPC in Louisiana, the LMHCA in
Washington, the LPC-Associate in Texas, the RMHCI in Florida, the LAC in
Arizona, and the LACMH in Delaware. New York breaks the pattern by name
only: it issues a Limited Permit rather than an "associate" title, but the
function is the same, supervised practice while you accrue hours.

**Stage three: the full license.** Once you hit the required hour total,
pass any remaining exam requirements, and get your supervised hours
verified, the board issues a full, independent-practice license. This is
the LCMHC, the LPCC, the LPC, the LMHC, or the LPCC again in Ohio's case
(more on that naming quirk below), depending on the state.

That three-stage shape is universal. What is not universal is anything
inside it: the hour totals, the supervision cadence, who is allowed to
supervise you, and whether the full license is actually the final stage or
just the second-to-last one.

## A real comparison, sourced from the rules

Here is how six states compare on the associate-to-full-license
conversion, pulled directly from AuditHalo's rule files for each
jurisdiction.

| State | Associate credential | Full license | Hours to convert |
|---|---|---|---|
| North Carolina | LCMHCA | LCMHC | 3,000 (100 supervision hours, 2,000+ direct contact) |
| California | APCC | LPCC | 3,000 (1,750+ direct contact) |
| Louisiana | PLPC | LPC | 3,000 (100 face-to-face supervision hours, 1,900+ direct) |
| Florida | RMHCI | LMHC | 1,500 (100 supervision hours, 1,000+ face-to-face) |
| Arizona | LAC | LPC | 3,200 (100 supervision hours, 1,600+ direct contact) |
| Delaware | LACMH | LPCMH | 3,200 (100 supervision hours, 1,500+ direct contact, 750+ individual) |

Every number above traces to a specific rule file: North Carolina's
3,000/100 split and 2,000-hour direct-contact minimum come from
`rules/nc-lcmhca/v1.yaml`, sourced to 21 NCAC 53 and N.C. Gen. Stat.
§90-330. California's 3,000-hour total and 1,750-hour direct-contact
minimum come from `rules/ca-apcc/v1.yaml`, sourced to Cal. Bus. & Prof.
Code §§4999.10 to 4999.46.3. Louisiana's numbers come from
`rules/la-plpc/v1.yaml`, sourced to LAC 46:LX and R.S. 37:1101 to 1123.
Florida's 1,500-hour total and 1,000-hour direct-contact figure come from
`rules/fl-rmhci/v1.yaml`, sourced to F.S. 491 and F.A.C. 64B4. Arizona's
3,200-hour total and 1,600-hour direct-contact minimum come from
`rules/az-lac/v1.yaml`, sourced to A.A.C. Title 4, Chapter 6. Delaware's
3,200-hour total, with the 1,500-hour direct-contact and 750-hour
individual-contact split, come from `rules/de-lacmh/v1.yaml`, sourced to
24 DE Admin. Code 3000 and 24 Del. C. §§3030 to 3034.

Two more states worth naming even though they didn't make the table above:
Texas requires 3,000 hours for its LPC-Associate, with at least 1,500
direct client contact, tracked in `rules/tx-lpc-associate/v1.yaml`.
Washington requires 3,000 hours for its LMHCA, with at least 1,200 direct
contact and a notably longer 36-month minimum duration, tracked in
`rules/wa-lmhca/v1.yaml`. Ohio and New York don't fit cleanly into an
"associate to full" table because their structures work differently:
Ohio's LPC is already a standalone license, not a temporary associate
credential, and its holders work toward the LPCC clinical add-on rather
than a first full license (`rules/oh-lpc/v1.yaml`). New York doesn't issue
an associate credential at all. It issues a Limited Permit that expires
rather than converts, and permit holders are barred from using any
credential abbreviation on professional materials while they hold it
(`rules/ny-lmhc-lp/v1.yaml`).

The spread in that table is the headline. Florida's 1,500-hour total is
less than half of Arizona's or Delaware's 3,200. If you're comparing your
own state's requirement to a friend's in another state and the numbers
don't match, that's not a mistake on either side. It's the actual design
of a system with no federal floor.

## A full license is not always the top of the ladder

Here's the part that trips people up most: earning your full license
doesn't automatically make you eligible to supervise someone else's
associate hours. In several states, supervising an associate requires a
separate, additional credential, earned only after a further stretch of
post-licensure experience.

North Carolina is the clearest example. An LCMHC (the full license) is not
automatically qualified to supervise an LCMHCA. Supervising requires the
LCMHCS credential, which requires at least two years of post-LCMHC
experience plus a board-approved supervisor training course, on top of the
full license itself. We cover this credential in detail in [our companion
post on LCMHCS](/blog/lcmhcs-north-carolina-supervisor-credential-explained),
and the actual timeline math, stage by stage, in [LCMHCA to LCMHCS: how
long does it actually take](/blog/lcmhca-to-lcmhcs-how-long-does-it-take).

Louisiana runs the same pattern under a different name. A Louisiana LPC
cannot supervise a PLPC's hours just by holding the LPC. Supervising
requires the LPC-S (LPC-Supervisor) designation, which requires a Louisiana
LPC in good standing, a minimum of three years of post-licensure
experience, and completion of either a 45-hour graduate course or a
25-hour approved training program, per `rules/la-plpc/v1.yaml`.

Texas layers this in too, with its own naming convention. A Texas LPC
cannot supervise an LPC-Associate's hours without the separate
LPC-Supervisor designation, which requires five years as a licensed LPC
plus a 40-hour board-approved supervisor training course, per
`rules/tx-lpc-associate/v1.yaml`.

Not every state adds this extra rung. New York's supervisor pool is
unusually broad by comparison: any active LMHC, LCSW, licensed
psychologist, licensed psychiatrist, or LMFT qualifies to supervise a
Limited Permit holder, with no separate supervisor-specific credential
required at all. Arizona and Delaware sit somewhere in between: both
require supervisors to complete a defined block of supervisor training,
but neither names a distinct, board-issued supervisor credential the way
North Carolina, Louisiana, and Texas do.

The practical takeaway: if you're a newly full-licensed counselor thinking
about taking on a supervisee, don't assume your license alone qualifies
you. Check whether your state requires a separate supervisor credential,
and if it does, find out exactly how many years of post-licensure
experience and what training the board expects before you agree to sign
off on anyone's hours.

## Why the differences matter more than the pattern

Knowing that every state follows the same three-stage shape is useful for
orienting yourself, but it won't get you licensed. The specific numbers
in your state are what actually govern whether your hours count. A
Louisiana PLPC who assumes their state works like Florida's 1,500-hour
system will badly undercount what they need. A newly licensed Texas LPC
who assumes their license alone qualifies them to supervise, the way it
does in New York, will find out the hard way that it doesn't.

This is also where a lot of otherwise well-intentioned supervision
relationships run into trouble. Getting the pattern right and the
specific numbers wrong is arguably worse than not knowing the pattern at
all, because it produces false confidence. If you want the full picture
